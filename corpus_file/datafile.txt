Mississippi's history: it begins thousands of years ago with Paleo-Indians who hunted large game after the last Ice Age, followed by mound-building cultures such as the Troyville, Coles Creek, and Plaquemine peoples, whose traditions blended with the upriver Mississippian culture around 1150 to 1350 CE and whose descendants include the historic Choctaw, Chickasaw, Natchez, and other tribes. The Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto crossed the region in the early 1540s, but lasting colonization came from the French, who founded Fort Maurepas near present-day Ocean Springs in 1699 and Fort Rosalie at Natchez in 1716, bringing Catholic missions and a small class of free people of color. After the Seven Years' War the area was divided between Spain and Britain, and in 1783 the Treaty of Paris gave most of it to the United States, though Spanish control lingered in the south until Pinckney's Treaty in 1795 and the Louisiana Purchase in 1803 settled river access. Mississippi Territory was created in 1798, and between then and 1820 its population jumped from under 9,000 to more than 222,000, most of them enslaved Africans brought by settlers lured by cheap land and the profits of cotton after the invention of the cotton gin. Mississippi became the 20th state on December 10, 1817, with Natchez as its first capital and Jackson taking over in 1822, while a series of treaties culminating in Dancing Rabbit Creek in 1830 and the Indian Removal Act forced most Choctaw and Chickasaw to leave for Indian Territory. By 1860 cotton output had grown past one million bales, the state was 55 percent Black and overwhelmingly enslaved, and a few thousand planters controlled its wealth and politics, so after Abraham Lincoln's election Mississippi seceded on January 9, 1861, and Senator Jefferson Davis became president of the Confederacy. More than 80,000 Mississippians fought, the state was central to the war because of the river, and Grant's campaign ended with the surrender of Vicksburg on July 4, 1863, while Unionist pockets like Jones County, the movement of slaves to Union lines, and the destruction of towns and railroads reshaped life. During Reconstruction, white officials first passed restrictive Black Codes, then Congress imposed military rule, and a new constitution in 1868 established universal male suffrage and public schools, allowing Black leaders such as Hiram Revels, Blanche K. Bruce, and John R. Lynch to hold high office and leading to Mississippi's readmission in 1870, but the Ku Klux Klan, the White League, the Red Shirts, the Vicksburg massacre of 1874, and the "Mississippi Plan" of 1875 used violence and fraud to return white Democrats to power and force out Governor Adelbert Ames in 1876. Afterward, conservative "Bourbon" Democrats ruled, the crop-lien system and falling cotton prices turned many farmers into sharecroppers, a yellow fever epidemic killed more than 3,000 in 1878, and the 1890 constitution with its poll tax cut Black voting by about 90 percent, a system upheld in Williams v. Mississippi in 1898 and followed by total Jim Crow segregation, lynchings, boll weevil and flood disasters, and the departure of hundreds of thousands of Black residents in two waves of the Great Migration between 1910 and 1960. Meanwhile, the state remained poor and rural, though it saw some progressive reforms under Governor Theodore Bilbo, modest prosperity in the 1920s, a new state highway system, the shift from cotton toward soybeans, livestock, and timber, and the mechanization after World War II that ended sharecropping, and it nurtured gospel, blues, jazz, and rock and roll, with artists like Muddy Waters, B.B. King, and Bo Diddley emerging from the Delta. After the Brown v. Board of Education decision in 1954, the state created the Sovereignty Commission and White Citizens Councils to resist integration, but activists in the NAACP, CORE, SNCC, and the Council of Federated Organizations pressed on, James Meredith integrated the University of Mississippi amid a deadly riot in 1962, Freedom Summer brought hundreds of volunteers in 1964, the murders of James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner galvanized the nation, and the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, with Fannie Lou Hamer's testimony, challenged the all-white delegation at the Democratic convention, helping bring about the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. In the decades since, Black Mississippians have entered politics mainly through the Democratic Party while white voters have moved toward the Republicans, who elected Kirk Fordice governor in 1991 and won the legislature in 2011, the legalization of riverboat gambling in 1990 gave the economy a boost, Hurricanes Camille in 1969 and Katrina in 2005 caused enormous destruction along the Gulf Coast, and today the state relies on agriculture, forestry, manufacturing, transportation, utilities, and health services, while remaining famous for writers such as William Faulkner, Eudora Welty, and John Grisham.
