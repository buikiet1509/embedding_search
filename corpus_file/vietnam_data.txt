Vietnam, officially the Socialist Republic of Vietnam (Cộng hòa Xã hội chủ nghĩa Việt Nam), is a country at the eastern edge of mainland Southeast Asia, on the Indochinese Peninsula. It covers about 331,000 square kilometres and had a population of a little over 100 million people in 2024, making it the 15th most populous country in the world and the third most populous in Southeast Asia after Indonesia and the Philippines.

Vietnam shares land borders with China to the north, and with Laos and Cambodia to the west. To the east and south it faces the South China Sea, which Vietnam calls the East Sea (Biển Đông). It also shares maritime boundaries with Thailand through the Gulf of Thailand, and with the Philippines, Indonesia and Malaysia across the South China Sea.

The capital is Hanoi (Hà Nội), in the north. The largest city and main economic centre is Ho Chi Minh City (Thành phố Hồ Chí Minh), formerly called Saigon, in the south. Other major cities include Hai Phong, Da Nang, Can Tho and Hue.

The official language is Vietnamese. The currency is the Vietnamese đồng (VND). The country uses the time zone UTC+07:00 and drives on the right. Its internet top-level domain is .vn and its international calling code is +84.

Vietnam is a one-party socialist republic led by the Communist Party of Vietnam. Since the Đổi Mới (Renovation) reforms of 1986, it has transformed from one of the poorest countries in the world into a lower-middle-income economy with one of the fastest growth rates in Asia. It is a member of the United Nations, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), the World Trade Organization (WTO), the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC), and the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP).

The name Việt Nam combines two Sino-Vietnamese elements. "Việt" refers to the Yue (Bách Việt), a broad term used in ancient Chinese texts for peoples living south of the Yangtze River. "Nam" means "south". In Vietnamese word order the name can be read as "the Viet of the South", while in Classical Chinese word order it can be read as "south of the Viet".

A similar name, Nam Việt (Nanyue in Chinese), was used for a kingdom founded in 204 BC by Zhao Tuo (Triệu Đà). The form "Việt Nam" appears in 16th-century writings, including poetry attributed to Nguyễn Bỉnh Khiêm. In 1804 Emperor Gia Long of the Nguyễn dynasty asked the Qing court in China to recognise the name Nam Việt; the Qing emperor reversed the order to Việt Nam to avoid confusion with the ancient kingdom. Emperor Minh Mạng later renamed the country Đại Nam ("Great South") in 1839.

During the French colonial period the country was usually called Annam by outsiders, and was divided into Tonkin (north), Annam (centre) and Cochinchina (south). The name Việt Nam was adopted officially by the nationalist movement and by both the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (1945) and the State of Vietnam (1949).

Historical names of states on Vietnamese territory include Văn Lang, Âu Lạc, Vạn Xuân, Đại Cồ Việt, Đại Việt, Đại Ngu and Đại Nam.

Archaeological evidence shows that humans lived in what is now Vietnam during the Paleolithic period. The Sơn Vi culture (around 20,000 BC) and the Hòa Bình culture (around 12,000–10,000 BC) are associated with hunter-gatherers. The Bắc Sơn culture followed in the Neolithic. Rice cultivation developed in the Red River valley in the second and first millennia BC. The Phùng Nguyên, Đồng Đậu and Gò Mun cultures preceded the Bronze Age.

The Đông Sơn culture, named after a village in Thanh Hóa province, flourished from about 1000 BC to the first centuries AD in northern Vietnam. It is famous for its large bronze drums, decorated with scenes of boats, birds, houses and people. The most famous example is the Ngọc Lũ drum.

According to Vietnamese tradition, the first state was Văn Lang, ruled by the Hùng kings of the Hồng Bàng dynasty. Vietnamese legend traces the people's origin to Lạc Long Quân, a dragon lord, and Âu Cơ, a fairy, who had one hundred children. Around 257 BC, Thục Phán (An Dương Vương) founded the kingdom of Âu Lạc, with its capital at Cổ Loa citadel near present-day Hanoi; the spiral-shaped citadel is one of the oldest fortifications in Vietnam.

In 179 BC, Âu Lạc was conquered by Zhao Tuo and incorporated into the kingdom of Nanyue (Nam Việt).

In 111 BC the Han dynasty of China conquered Nanyue, beginning about a thousand years of Chinese rule over northern Vietnam, a period Vietnamese historians call "Bắc thuộc" (northern domination). During this time Chinese administration, Confucianism, Chinese characters and Mahayana Buddhism spread in the region.

There were many uprisings. In 40 AD the Trưng sisters, Trưng Trắc and Trưng Nhị, led a rebellion against the Han and ruled briefly until they were defeated in 43 AD by the general Ma Yuan. In 248 AD Lady Triệu (Bà Triệu) led another revolt. In 544 Lý Bí founded the short-lived Early Lý dynasty and the state of Vạn Xuân. Mai Thúc Loan rebelled in 722, and Phùng Hưng in the late 8th century.

In 938 Ngô Quyền defeated the forces of the Southern Han at the Battle of Bạch Đằng River by planting iron-tipped stakes in the riverbed, which impaled enemy ships at low tide. In 939 he proclaimed himself king, ending Chinese domination.

After Ngô Quyền's death the country fell into the "Anarchy of the 12 Warlords". Đinh Bộ Lĩnh reunified it in 968, founding the Đinh dynasty and naming the country Đại Cồ Việt, with its capital at Hoa Lư. The Early Lê dynasty (980–1009), founded by Lê Hoàn, defeated a Song invasion in 981.

The Lý dynasty (1009–1225) was founded by Lý Thái Tổ, who moved the capital to Thăng Long (modern Hanoi) in 1010. The country was renamed Đại Việt in 1054. In 1070 the Temple of Literature (Văn Miếu) was built, and in 1076 the Imperial Academy (Quốc Tử Giám), considered Vietnam's first university, was founded. The general Lý Thường Kiệt defeated the Song dynasty in 1075–1077; the poem "Nam quốc sơn hà" from this war is often called Vietnam's first declaration of independence.

The Trần dynasty (1225–1400) repelled three Mongol invasions of the Yuan dynasty, in 1258, 1285 and 1287–1288. The commander Trần Hưng Đạo won the decisive third Battle of Bạch Đằng in 1288, again using stakes in the river. The Trần period saw the development of chữ Nôm, a script for writing Vietnamese using modified Chinese characters.

The Hồ dynasty (1400–1407) of Hồ Quý Ly was short-lived; the Ming dynasty of China invaded in 1407 and ruled Vietnam until 1427. Lê Lợi led the Lam Sơn uprising from 1418 and expelled the Ming in 1427. He founded the Later Lê dynasty (1428–1789). Nguyễn Trãi wrote "Bình Ngô đại cáo" (Great Proclamation upon the Pacification of the Wu) to mark the victory. Under Emperor Lê Thánh Tông (reigned 1460–1497), the Hồng Đức legal code was issued and Đại Việt conquered much of the Champa kingdom in 1471.

Over several centuries the Vietnamese expanded south along the coast, a process called Nam tiến ("march to the south"). They absorbed the territories of the Champa kingdom, an Indianised Hindu-Buddhist civilisation in central Vietnam known for its brick towers such as Mỹ Sơn, and later took the Mekong Delta from the Khmer Empire during the 17th and 18th centuries.

In 1527 Mạc Đăng Dung seized the throne, founding the Mạc dynasty, and civil war followed. The Lê dynasty was restored in name, but real power was divided between two families: the Trịnh lords in the north (Đàng Ngoài) and the Nguyễn lords in the south (Đàng Trong). They fought a long war from 1627 to 1672, and the Gianh River became the border between their realms.

In 1771 the Tây Sơn rebellion began, led by three brothers: Nguyễn Nhạc, Nguyễn Huệ and Nguyễn Lữ. They overthrew both the Nguyễn and Trịnh lords. Nguyễn Huệ, as Emperor Quang Trung, defeated a Siamese army at Rạch Gầm–Xoài Mút in 1785 and a Qing Chinese army at Ngọc Hồi–Đống Đa in early 1789.

Nguyễn Phúc Ánh, a survivor of the Nguyễn lords, defeated the Tây Sơn with help from French volunteers and became Emperor Gia Long in 1802. He founded the Nguyễn dynasty (1802–1945), the last imperial dynasty, and established its capital at Huế. The Imperial City of Huế is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Emperor Minh Mạng (reigned 1820–1841) centralised the state and restricted Christian missionaries.

European traders and missionaries arrived from the 16th century. The Jesuit Alexandre de Rhodes helped develop chữ Quốc ngữ, the Latin-based alphabet now used for Vietnamese, publishing a Vietnamese–Portuguese–Latin dictionary in 1651.

In 1858 France and Spain attacked Đà Nẵng, citing the persecution of Catholics. France took Saigon in 1859 and the three eastern provinces of Cochinchina by the Treaty of Saigon in 1862, and the rest of Cochinchina in 1867. After the Tonkin campaign and the Sino-French War (1884–1885), France established protectorates over Annam and Tonkin by the Patenôtre Treaty of 1884. In 1887 French Indochina was created, including Cochinchina, Annam, Tonkin and Cambodia; Laos was added in 1893.

The colonial economy relied on rice exports from the Mekong Delta, rubber plantations, coal mining and monopolies on salt, alcohol and opium. Resistance movements included the Cần Vương movement (from 1885), the Đông Du movement of Phan Bội Châu, the reform efforts of Phan Châu Trinh, the Vietnamese Nationalist Party (Việt Nam Quốc dân Đảng), which led the failed Yên Bái mutiny in 1930, and the Indochinese Communist Party, founded by Hồ Chí Minh in 1930.

Japan occupied French Indochina from 1940, leaving the Vichy French administration in place until March 1945, when Japan overthrew it and created the Empire of Vietnam under Emperor Bảo Đại. The Việt Minh, a communist-led independence front founded in 1941, gained support in the north. A famine in 1944–1945 killed an estimated one to two million people in northern Vietnam.

After Japan's surrender, the Việt Minh seized power in the August Revolution. On 2 September 1945, Hồ Chí Minh read the Declaration of Independence at Ba Đình Square in Hanoi, proclaiming the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. Bảo Đại abdicated. 2 September is Vietnam's National Day.

France sought to restore its colony, and war broke out in December 1946. France created the State of Vietnam in 1949 with Bảo Đại as head of state. The Việt Minh, led militarily by General Võ Nguyên Giáp and supported by China and the Soviet Union, defeated the French at the Battle of Điện Biên Phủ, which ended on 7 May 1954.

The Geneva Accords of 1954 divided Vietnam temporarily at the 17th parallel, with the Democratic Republic of Vietnam in the north and the State of Vietnam in the south, pending national elections in 1956 that were never held. About one million people, many of them Catholics, moved from north to south.

In 1955 Ngô Đình Diệm deposed Bảo Đại and founded the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam), with its capital at Saigon. North Vietnam pursued land reform and supported an insurgency in the south led by the National Liberation Front (known to its opponents as the Viet Cong), formed in 1960. Supplies moved south along the Hồ Chí Minh Trail through Laos and Cambodia.

Diệm was killed in a coup in November 1963. After the Gulf of Tonkin incident in August 1964, the United States escalated its involvement, beginning sustained bombing of the north (Operation Rolling Thunder, 1965–1968) and deploying combat troops; US troop levels peaked at about 540,000 in 1969. South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, Thailand and the Philippines also sent forces.

The Tết Offensive of 1968 was a military setback for the communists but a political turning point, weakening US public support for the war. The My Lai massacre took place in March 1968. Hồ Chí Minh died on 2 September 1969. Under "Vietnamization", US forces withdrew gradually. The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973, and the last US combat troops left that year.

In 1975 North Vietnam launched a final offensive. Saigon fell on 30 April 1975, now commemorated as Reunification Day. Estimates of total deaths in the war range from about 1.3 million to over 3 million, including civilians. The United States lost about 58,000 service members.

Vietnam was formally reunified on 2 July 1976 as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam, and Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City. Hundreds of thousands of former South Vietnamese officials and soldiers were sent to re-education camps. Collectivisation and nationalisation of private business, together with war damage and a US embargo, led to severe economic hardship. Between 1975 and the mid-1990s, more than a million people fled by sea as "boat people", along with others who left overland.

Vietnam invaded Cambodia in December 1978 and overthrew the Khmer Rouge regime in January 1979, occupying the country until 1989. In February–March 1979 China invaded northern Vietnam in the Sino-Vietnamese War, and border clashes continued through the 1980s. In 1988 the two countries fought a naval clash at Johnson South Reef in the Spratly Islands.

At the Sixth Party Congress in December 1986, the Communist Party launched Đổi Mới ("Renovation"), shifting toward a "socialist-oriented market economy". Agriculture was decollectivised, private enterprise was allowed, and foreign investment was encouraged. Vietnam went from importing rice to becoming one of the world's largest rice exporters within a few years.

Vietnam normalised relations with China in 1991, joined ASEAN in 1995, and normalised diplomatic relations with the United States in 1995 after the US lifted its trade embargo in 1994. It joined the WTO in 2007. Relations with the United States were upgraded to a comprehensive strategic partnership in 2023.

Nguyễn Phú Trọng served as General Secretary of the Communist Party from 2011 until his death in July 2024 and led a long anti-corruption campaign known as the "blazing furnace" (đốt lò). Tô Lâm, formerly Minister of Public Security, became president in May 2024 and General Secretary in August 2024; Lương Cường became president in October 2024. In 2025 Vietnam carried out a major administrative reorganisation, merging ministries, abolishing the district level of government and reducing the number of provinces and centrally run cities from 63 to 34, effective 1 July 2025.

Vietnam is long and narrow, shaped like the letter S. It stretches about 1,650 kilometres from north to south, but at its narrowest point, in Quảng Bình province, it is only about 50 kilometres wide. The coastline is about 3,260 kilometres long, excluding islands. Land borders total about 4,600 kilometres: roughly 1,300 km with China, 2,100 km with Laos and 1,200 km with Cambodia.

Vietnam is traditionally divided into three regions: the North (Bắc Bộ), the Centre (Trung Bộ) and the South (Nam Bộ). These are often subdivided into the Northwest, Northeast, Red River Delta, North Central Coast, South Central Coast, Central Highlands (Tây Nguyên), Southeast and Mekong Delta.

Hills and mountains cover about three-quarters of the country. The Hoàng Liên Sơn range in the northwest contains Fansipan, at 3,143 metres the highest mountain in Vietnam and in the whole of Indochina; it is sometimes called "the roof of Indochina". The Trường Sơn range (Annamite Range) runs along the border with Laos. The Central Highlands are a plateau region of basalt soils well suited to coffee, rubber and pepper.

The two main lowland areas are the Red River Delta (Đồng bằng sông Hồng) in the north, with an area of about 15,000 km², and the Mekong River Delta (Đồng bằng sông Cửu Long) in the south, with about 40,000 km². The Mekong Delta is Vietnam's "rice bowl", producing more than half of the country's rice.

The Red River (Sông Hồng) rises in Yunnan, China, and flows about 1,150 km to the Gulf of Tonkin; its reddish colour comes from iron-rich sediment. The Mekong (Mê Kông) enters Vietnam from Cambodia and splits into many branches before reaching the sea; Vietnamese call it Cửu Long, "Nine Dragons", after its mouths. Other important rivers include the Đà, Lô, Mã, Cả, Hương (Perfume River, in Huế), Đồng Nai and Sài Gòn rivers.

Vietnam has thousands of islands. Phú Quốc, in the Gulf of Thailand, is the largest, at about 575 km². Other notable islands include Côn Đảo, Cát Bà, Lý Sơn and Cù Lao Chàm. Vietnam claims the Paracel Islands (Hoàng Sa), which China has controlled since 1974, and the Spratly Islands (Trường Sa), parts of which are also claimed by China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Malaysia and Brunei.

Hạ Long Bay in Quảng Ninh province contains about 1,600 limestone islands and islets and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Famous beaches include Mỹ Khê in Đà Nẵng, Nha Trang, Mũi Né and Phú Quốc.

Phong Nha-Kẻ Bàng National Park in Quảng Bình province is a UNESCO World Heritage Site with hundreds of caves. Hang Sơn Đoòng, discovered by a local man, Hồ Khanh, around 1990 and explored by a British-Vietnamese team in 2009, is the largest known cave passage in the world by volume.

Vietnam lies entirely in the tropics but its climate varies considerably because of its length and terrain. The North has a humid subtropical climate with four seasons: a cool, dry winter (December to February, when Hanoi can fall below 10 °C), a warm spring, a hot and rainy summer, and autumn. Sapa and mountainous areas occasionally see frost and even snow.

The South has a tropical savanna climate with two seasons: a rainy season from about May to November and a dry season from December to April. Ho Chi Minh City stays warm all year, with average temperatures between about 26 °C and 29 °C.

The central coast has its rainy season later, from about September to December, when typhoons and floods are most frequent. The Central Highlands are cooler because of their altitude; Đà Lạt, at about 1,500 metres, has a mild climate year-round and is known as the "city of eternal spring".

Annual rainfall ranges from about 1,200 mm to over 3,000 mm. Vietnam is one of the countries most affected by typhoons in the region; Typhoon Yagi in September 2024 was among the strongest storms ever to hit northern Vietnam. Vietnam is considered highly vulnerable to climate change, especially sea-level rise and saltwater intrusion in the Mekong Delta.

Vietnam is one of the most biologically diverse countries in the world, with around 16,000 plant species and more than 300 mammal species, about 850 bird species, and hundreds of reptile, amphibian and freshwater fish species. Many species were discovered only recently, including the saola (Pseudoryx nghetinhensis), a forest bovine found in the Annamite Range in 1992 and one of the rarest large mammals on Earth, the giant muntjac and the Truong Son muntjac.

Notable endangered animals include the Tonkin snub-nosed monkey, the Delacour's langur, the red-shanked douc, the Indochinese tiger, the Asian elephant, the Sunda pangolin and the Hoan Kiem turtle (Rafetus swinhoei). The Javan rhinoceros became extinct in Vietnam in 2010 when the last individual was killed by poachers in Cát Tiên National Park.

Vietnam has more than 30 national parks, including Cúc Phương (the first, established in 1962), Cát Tiên, Ba Bể, Bạch Mã, Phong Nha-Kẻ Bàng, Côn Đảo, U Minh Thượng and Tràm Chim. The Cần Giờ mangrove forest near Ho Chi Minh City is a UNESCO biosphere reserve.

Environmental problems include deforestation, illegal wildlife trade, air pollution in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, plastic waste, river pollution and sand mining in the Mekong. During the Vietnam War, the US military sprayed about 80 million litres of herbicides, including Agent Orange, which contained dioxin; the effects on health and the environment continue. Unexploded ordnance still contaminates large areas, especially in Quảng Trị province. The 2016 Formosa Ha Tinh steel plant toxic spill killed large numbers of fish along the central coast.

Vietnam is a one-party state. The constitution of 2013 describes the Communist Party of Vietnam (Đảng Cộng sản Việt Nam) as "the force leading the State and society". The party holds a National Congress every five years, which elects the Central Committee; the Central Committee elects the Politburo and the General Secretary. The General Secretary is the most powerful position in the country. The 13th National Congress took place in 2021 and the 14th in January 2026.

The country's leadership has traditionally been described as a collective of "four pillars": the General Secretary of the Party, the President, the Prime Minister and the Chairman of the National Assembly.

The National Assembly (Quốc hội) is the highest organ of state power and the unicameral legislature, with up to 500 deputies elected for five-year terms. Candidates are vetted by the Vietnam Fatherland Front, an umbrella organisation linked to the party; the large majority of deputies are party members.

The President (Chủ tịch nước) is head of state and commander-in-chief, elected by the National Assembly. The Prime Minister (Thủ tướng) heads the government and is also elected by the National Assembly. Phạm Minh Chính became prime minister in 2021. The judicial system is headed by the Supreme People's Court, and the Supreme People's Procuracy supervises law enforcement.

International human-rights organisations criticise Vietnam for restrictions on freedom of speech, press, assembly and religion, for imprisoning bloggers and activists, and for the absence of independent trade unions. Vietnam retains the death penalty for a range of crimes, including drug trafficking and some economic crimes; in 2024 property tycoon Trương Mỹ Lan was sentenced to death in a massive fraud case involving Saigon Commercial Bank. Same-sex marriage is not legally recognised, but in 2015 Vietnam removed its ban on same-sex wedding ceremonies, and it allows legal gender change under some conditions.

Until mid-2025 Vietnam was divided into 58 provinces (tỉnh) and 5 centrally governed cities (thành phố trực thuộc trung ương): Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Hai Phong, Da Nang and Can Tho. Below them were districts and then communes, wards and townships.

Under the 2025 reform, the number of provincial-level units was reduced to 34 (28 provinces and 6 centrally governed cities, with Huế becoming the sixth), and the district level was abolished, leaving a two-tier system of provinces and communes. Some notable mergers included the combination of Ho Chi Minh City with Bình Dương and Bà Rịa–Vũng Tàu, and of Da Nang with Quảng Nam.

Vietnam follows a foreign policy of "independence, self-reliance, multilateralisation and diversification", sometimes summarised as "bamboo diplomacy": firm roots but flexible branches. Its "Four Nos" defence policy rules out military alliances, siding with one country against another, foreign military bases on its soil, and the use or threat of force in international relations.

Vietnam has comprehensive strategic partnerships with China, Russia, India, South Korea, the United States, Japan, Australia, France and several other countries. China is its largest trading partner, and the United States is its largest export market. Relations with China are shaped by the dispute over the South China Sea. Vietnam has hosted major summits, including the APEC summit in 2006 and 2017 and the second Trump–Kim summit in Hanoi in 2019. It was a non-permanent member of the UN Security Council in 2008–2009 and 2020–2021, and chaired ASEAN in 2010 and 2020.

The Vietnam People's Army (Quân đội nhân dân Việt Nam) includes ground forces, a navy, an air force, border guards and a coast guard. It has about 450,000–500,000 active personnel and millions of reservists and militia. Military service is compulsory for men, typically for 24 months. Russia has historically been the main arms supplier.

Vietnam has a socialist-oriented market economy. Its gross domestic product (GDP) was about US$476 billion in 2024, with growth of about 7.1% that year. GDP per capita is about US$4,700 nominal. Between 1990 and 2020, Vietnam averaged roughly 6–7% annual growth, and the poverty rate fell from over 50% in the early 1990s to a few percent. The government aims to become a high-income country by 2045, the 100th anniversary of independence.

State-owned enterprises remain important in energy, banking, telecommunications and transport; major ones include Petrovietnam, Electricity of Vietnam (EVN), Vietnam Airlines, Viettel (owned by the Ministry of Defence) and Vinacomin. Large private conglomerates include Vingroup (founded by Phạm Nhật Vượng, Vietnam's richest person), Masan, FPT, Hòa Phát and VietJet Air.

Vietnam is one of the world's most trade-dependent economies, with total trade larger than its GDP. Main exports include electronics and phones, computers and components, textiles and garments, footwear, machinery, wood and furniture, seafood, rice, coffee and cashews. Samsung Electronics is the largest single foreign investor and produces a large share of its smartphones in Bắc Ninh and Thái Nguyên provinces. Other major manufacturers include Intel, LG, Foxconn, Nike and Adidas suppliers, and Apple suppliers. Vietnam benefited from the "China plus one" strategy as companies diversified supply chains.

Free-trade agreements include the ASEAN Free Trade Area, the CPTPP (2019), the EU–Vietnam Free Trade Agreement (EVFTA, 2020), the UK–Vietnam FTA (2021) and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP, 2022).

Agriculture employs a large share of the workforce, although its share of GDP has fallen to around 12%. Vietnam is one of the world's top exporters of rice, the second-largest producer and exporter of coffee after Brazil (mainly robusta, grown in the Central Highlands, especially Đắk Lắk), the largest exporter of cashew nuts and black pepper, and a major exporter of seafood, including pangasius (tra and basa catfish) and shrimp. Other products include rubber, tea, cassava, durian, dragon fruit and other tropical fruit.

Electricity comes mainly from coal, hydropower and gas, with fast-growing solar and wind. The Hòa Bình and Sơn La dams are among the largest hydropower plants in Southeast Asia. Vietnam had a solar boom around 2019–2020, especially in Ninh Thuận and Bình Thuận provinces. It has committed to net-zero emissions by 2050. Offshore oil and gas fields include Bạch Hổ (White Tiger), and the Dung Quất and Nghi Sơn refineries process crude oil.

Tourism is a major industry. Vietnam received about 18 million international visitors in 2019 and about 17.5 million in 2024, with the largest numbers from China and South Korea. Popular destinations include Hanoi, Hạ Long Bay, Sa Pa, Ninh Bình (Tràng An), Huế, Hội An, Đà Nẵng, Nha Trang, Đà Lạt, Ho Chi Minh City, the Mekong Delta and Phú Quốc.

The information technology sector has grown rapidly, with firms such as FPT, Viettel and VNPT. The game Flappy Bird, created by Vietnamese developer Nguyễn Hà Đông in 2013, became a global phenomenon. Vietnam's first satellite, Vinasat-1, was launched in 2008. The mathematician Ngô Bảo Châu won the Fields Medal in 2010 for his proof of the fundamental lemma in the Langlands program.

The North–South Expressway is being built along the length of the country. National Route 1 (Quốc lộ 1A) runs from the Chinese border to Cà Mau. The North–South Railway (Reunification Express) connects Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City over about 1,726 km; a high-speed railway between the two cities was approved in 2024.

Hanoi opened its first urban rail line, Cát Linh–Hà Đông, in 2021, and Ho Chi Minh City opened Metro Line 1 (Bến Thành–Suối Tiên) in December 2024.

The main international airports are Nội Bài (Hanoi), Tân Sơn Nhất (Ho Chi Minh City), Đà Nẵng, Cam Ranh and Phú Quốc. Long Thành International Airport is under construction in Đồng Nai to serve the Ho Chi Minh City region. Major seaports include Hải Phòng, Cái Mép–Thị Vải and Cát Lái.

Motorbikes are the dominant form of personal transport, with tens of millions registered; Honda and Yamaha are the most common brands. VinFast, a subsidiary of Vingroup, produces electric cars and scooters.

Vietnam's population passed 100 million in 2023. The population density is about 310 people per km², with the highest densities in the Red River Delta and around Ho Chi Minh City. About 38–40% of people live in urban areas. The fertility rate has fallen below replacement level (about 1.9 children per woman), and the population is ageing quickly. A two-child policy was in place for decades; in 2025 the limit was abolished.

Vietnam officially recognises 54 ethnic groups. The Kinh (Việt) make up about 85% of the population. The largest minority groups are the Tày, Thái, Mường, Hoa (ethnic Chinese), Khmer, Nùng and Hmông. Most minorities live in the northern mountains, the Central Highlands and the Mekong Delta. The Central Highlands are home to groups such as the Ê Đê, Gia Rai and Ba Na, and the Cham live mainly in Ninh Thuận and An Giang.

Ho Chi Minh City (about 9–10 million before the 2025 merger; around 14 million after), Hanoi (about 8.5 million), Hai Phong, Can Tho, Da Nang, Biên Hòa, Huế, Nha Trang and Vinh.

Vietnamese (tiếng Việt) is the official and national language. It is an Austroasiatic language of the Vietic branch, related to Mường. It is tonal: the northern standard has six tones (ngang, huyền, sắc, hỏi, ngã, nặng), while southern dialects merge the hỏi and ngã tones. There are three main dialect regions: Northern (Hanoi), Central (Huế) and Southern (Saigon). A large share of vocabulary comes from Chinese (Sino-Vietnamese words), and there are many French loanwords such as "cà phê" (coffee), "bánh mì" (bread) and "ga" (station).

Vietnamese was historically written in Classical Chinese (chữ Hán) and in chữ Nôm. The Latin-based chữ Quốc ngữ, using diacritics to mark tones and vowels, became dominant in the early 20th century and is now the only official script. Minority languages include Tày, Thái, Hmông, Khmer and Cham. English is the most widely studied foreign language; French, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Russian are also taught.

Most Vietnamese practise folk religion or are not formally affiliated with any organised religion; ancestor worship is nearly universal. Mahayana Buddhism is the largest organised religion. Catholicism is followed by about 6–7% of the population, one of the highest shares in Asia. Other religions include Protestantism, Hòa Hảo (a Buddhist movement founded in 1939 in the Mekong Delta), Cao Đài (a syncretic religion founded in 1926 in Tây Ninh), Islam (mainly among the Cham) and Hinduism (also among some Cham). Confucianism and Taoism have strongly influenced Vietnamese ethics and customs.

Education is compulsory through primary and lower-secondary levels. The school system has 12 grades. The national high school graduation exam is a major annual event. Vietnamese students have performed strongly in the OECD's PISA tests compared with countries of similar income. Leading universities include Vietnam National University, Hanoi; Vietnam National University, Ho Chi Minh City; Hanoi University of Science and Technology; and the private VinUniversity and Fulbright University Vietnam. The literacy rate is about 96%.

Life expectancy is about 74 years. Vietnam has a public health insurance scheme covering more than 90% of the population. It was praised for its early response to COVID-19 in 2020, and in 2003 it was the first country to contain SARS. The leading causes of death are non-communicable diseases such as stroke and heart disease.

Vietnamese culture has been shaped by indigenous Southeast Asian traditions, about a millennium of Chinese influence, Confucian family values, Buddhism, and French and later American influences. Family, respect for elders, and ancestor veneration are central. Most homes have an ancestral altar.

The most important festival is Tết Nguyên Đán, the Lunar New Year, usually in late January or February. Families clean their homes, visit relatives, pay respects to ancestors, give lucky money (lì xì) in red envelopes, and eat traditional foods such as bánh chưng (square sticky rice cake) and bánh tét. Other festivals include the Mid-Autumn Festival (Tết Trung Thu), with lanterns and mooncakes for children; the Hùng Kings' Commemoration Day (10th day of the 3rd lunar month); and the Perfume Pagoda festival.

Public holidays include New Year's Day (1 January), Tết, Hùng Kings' Day, Reunification Day (30 April), International Workers' Day (1 May) and National Day (2 September).

Classical Vietnamese literature was written in Chinese and chữ Nôm. "The Tale of Kiều" (Truyện Kiều) by Nguyễn Du (1765–1820), a verse novel of 3,254 lines in the lục bát (six-eight) metre, is considered the masterpiece of Vietnamese literature. Hồ Xuân Hương, an 18th–19th century poet, is known for witty, double-meaning poems in Nôm. Modern writers include Nam Cao, Vũ Trọng Phụng, Nguyễn Huy Thiệp, Bảo Ninh ("The Sorrow of War") and Dương Thu Hương.

Traditional performing arts include water puppetry (múa rối nước), which originated in the Red River Delta and uses a pool of water as a stage; quan họ folk singing from Bắc Ninh; ca trù, a form of chamber music sung by women; nhã nhạc, the court music of Huế; đờn ca tài tử of the south; chèo and tuồng theatre; and cải lương, a modern folk opera from the south. Several of these are recognised by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage.

Traditional instruments include the đàn bầu (monochord), đàn tranh (16-string zither), đàn nguyệt (moon lute), đàn nhị (two-string fiddle) and the đàn đá (stone lithophone). Modern popular music (V-pop) is very popular; Sơn Tùng M-TP is one of the best-known artists. Trịnh Công Sơn was a celebrated songwriter of the war era.

Visual arts include lacquer painting (sơn mài), silk painting, Đông Hồ woodblock prints and ceramics from Bát Tràng village.

The áo dài, a long tunic worn over trousers, is the national dress, especially for women; it is worn as a school uniform in some high schools and on formal occasions. The nón lá, a conical palm-leaf hat, is a national symbol.

Vietnamese cuisine emphasises fresh herbs, vegetables, rice and a balance of flavours: salty, sweet, sour, bitter and spicy. Fish sauce (nước mắm) is the essential seasoning; Phú Quốc and Phan Thiết are famous for it.

Famous dishes include:
Phở: a noodle soup with beef (phở bò) or chicken (phở gà), originating in northern Vietnam in the early 20th century, likely around Nam Định and Hanoi.
Bánh mì: a baguette sandwich filled with pâté, cold cuts, pickled vegetables, coriander and chilli, a legacy of French influence.
Bún chả: grilled pork with rice vermicelli and dipping sauce, a Hanoi specialty; Barack Obama ate it with Anthony Bourdain in Hanoi in 2016.
Gỏi cuốn: fresh spring rolls with shrimp, pork, herbs and vermicelli wrapped in rice paper.
Chả giò / nem rán: fried spring rolls.
Bún bò Huế: a spicy beef noodle soup from Huế with lemongrass.
Cơm tấm: broken rice with grilled pork, a Saigon staple.
Cao lầu: a noodle dish unique to Hội An.
Mì Quảng: a turmeric noodle dish from Quảng Nam.
Bánh xèo: a crispy turmeric pancake filled with shrimp and bean sprouts.
Cà phê sữa đá: strong drip coffee with sweetened condensed milk over ice; egg coffee (cà phê trứng) is a Hanoi specialty created in 1946.

Football (soccer) is the most popular sport. The national team won the AFF Championship (ASEAN Football Championship) in 2008, 2018 and 2024–25, and the under-23 team reached the final of the AFC U-23 Championship in 2018. Traditional martial arts include Vovinam and Vietnamese traditional martial arts (võ cổ truyền). Other popular sports include badminton, volleyball, table tennis and đá cầu (shuttlecock kicking). Hoàng Xuân Vinh won Vietnam's first Olympic gold medal, in shooting (10 m air pistol), at the 2016 Rio de Janeiro Olympics. Vietnam hosted the Southeast Asian Games in 2003 and 2021 (held in 2022).

Major media outlets are state-owned or controlled, including Vietnam Television (VTV), Voice of Vietnam (VOV), the Vietnam News Agency, Nhân Dân (the party newspaper), Tuổi Trẻ, Thanh Niên and VnExpress. Facebook, YouTube and the domestic platform Zalo are widely used.

Flag: a red field with a yellow five-pointed star in the centre (cờ đỏ sao vàng). Red stands for revolution and bloodshed; the star's five points are often said to represent workers, peasants, soldiers, intellectuals and youth.
National anthem: "Tiến Quân Ca" ("Army March"), composed by Văn Cao in 1944.
Emblem: a red circle with a yellow star above a cogwheel and rice ears, with the country's name on a ribbon.
National motto: "Độc lập – Tự do – Hạnh phúc" ("Independence – Freedom – Happiness").
Unofficial national flower: the lotus (hoa sen).
National hero: Hồ Chí Minh, often called "Bác Hồ" (Uncle Ho); his embalmed body lies in the Hồ Chí Minh Mausoleum in Ba Đình Square, Hanoi.
